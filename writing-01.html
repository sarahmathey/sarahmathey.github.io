To Defend Democracy, We Must Regulate Technology

A policy memo to House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries arguing that decades of laissez-faire technology policy have privatized power in ways that threaten democratic governance — and outlining concrete steps to reassert it.

Type Policy Memo
To House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries
Date November 25, 2025

Executive Summary

Decades of laissez-faire technology policy and outsourcing government functions has led to the privatization of power in the hands of a few technology companies, threatening democratic governance and national sovereignty. The US government must act to reassert its authority. Reforming campaign finance law, banning stock trading by members of Congress, exploiting the government's purchasing power to demand transparency, and rebuilding technological expertise within the government are crucial first steps to reasserting government authority.

Background

In Tech Coup: How to Save Democracy from Silicon Valley, former EU parliamentarian and technology policy expert Marietje Schaake details the many ways in which the US government has ceded power to private companies by outsourcing critical government functions, from administering elections to managing cybersecurity, privatizing power and threatening democratic governance and national security.

The United States government has willfully ceded power to private companies by outsourcing critical government functions.

Private ownership of critical infrastructure — from traditional utility plants to the modern infrastructure that undergirds the digital economy: fiber optic cables, microchip manufacturing, and data centers — has eroded the government's ability to secure both national security and economic stability.

In a world increasingly utilizing cyberattacks in pursuit of geopolitical goals, private ownership of critical infrastructure leaves the US government unable to guarantee the security of the American people. Beyond cyberwarfare, Big Tech has made itself a central actor in the Russia-Ukraine war in ways that undermine Ukrainian sovereignty and US foreign policy.

Decades of limited regulation has left unchecked power in the hands of private companies.

Today, the financial resources of Big Tech firms eclipse those of large countries, allowing private companies to overwhelm the abilities of regulatory agencies to enforce existing laws and frame the narrative around policy debates. In the United States, Big Tech has invested untold time and money into the narrative that regulation stifles innovation and threatens global competitiveness. They can do this not only through direct lobbying efforts, but also by strategically placing former employees in privately sponsored government fellowships and funding think tanks. As we saw with the TikTok ban, these private companies also use their platforms to influence public opinion around legislation. The ability to influence public opinion through their platforms stretches beyond legislative debates, directly influencing public perceptions of candidates for public office through Super PAC spending and control over social media algorithms.

Even in the EU, the global leader in technology regulation, the financial resources of Big Tech firms have precluded enforcement of their landmark consumer privacy legislation. Regulatory agencies with budgets in the tens of millions are Davids facing the Goliaths of Big Tech with multi-billion dollar budgets. These dynamics have historically undermined anti-trust efforts in the United States as well.

This privatization of power undermines democracy.

Democratic governance is predicated on the public's ability to hold elected officials accountable for actions that affect their day-to-day lives. Accountability is dependent on transparency, but Big Tech firms exploit current intellectual property laws to limit public oversight of their products. When government services employ Big Tech products to provide public services — such as surveillance tools used in policing, or other algorithmically driven products — the public is unable to oversee what factors are driving decisions around public safety or the distribution of public services. Moreover, outsourcing government functions to private companies allows public officials to evade accountability by blaming private companies for any issues. When the public is unable to understand how decisions that impact their lives are made, we are unable to hold decision makers accountable or craft laws to make change. Between hiding behind intellectual property laws to overwhelming regulators when legal cases are brought against them, Big Tech functions beyond the rule of law.

Moreover, Big Tech firms have directly threatened sovereign nations dependent on their services to act in ways that are favorable to their bottom lines, not the public good. Elon Musk's insertion of himself as an actor in the Russia-Ukraine war through Starlink is a prime example of this. Other Big Tech firms have threatened to leave countries that pass regulations they don't like, threatening public access to now-essential digital tools and national tax bases that fund critical public services. When public officials are unable to hold private corporations accountable for their actions, it's time to question who is really in charge of decisions that are foundational to national security and economic stability — and if it's not elected officials, we are no longer living in a democracy.

Policy Recommendations

1. Congress must reform campaign finance laws, including reversing Citizens United, to diminish private companies' ability to influence policy makers. In the 2023–2024 election cycle, cryptocurrency Super PACs spent tens of millions of dollars to elect pro-crypto candidates. Elon Musk personally spent hundreds of millions in support of President Trump. In contrast, I spent $240 in support of federal candidates. When technology companies and their executives have such outsize influence on who is elected to office, elected officials will be accountable to these donors, not the public. It is difficult to imagine comprehensive legislation being passed to regulate Big Tech given these incentives.

2. Ban stock trading by members of Congress to decouple the private interests of policy makers and companies. Members of Congress must not be allowed to tie their financial security to those of companies they are responsible for regulating. Big Tech will always prioritize profit over the public good. Public officials must not be incentivized to do the same.

3. Exploit the government's purchasing power to demand transparency from technology companies. The US government spends billions of dollars purchasing products and services from Big Tech firms and must use this purchasing power to demand greater transparency. Requiring mandatory disclosure of all cybersecurity incidents or the publication of datasets used to train algorithmic tools should be standard clauses in procurement contracts. Companies with public contracts must also be accountable to FOIA requests when their products are used by public servants. No more hiding behind intellectual property law.

4. Rebuild technology capacity in government. The US government must rebuild its scientific and technological capacity to achieve independence from Big Tech. Initially, this means recruiting new, and training existing, public servants to be better able to hold private tech companies to account, and fully funding the efforts of regulatory agencies such as the FTC. In many cases, we do not need new laws to curb Big Tech — we need to be able to enforce the laws we already have. In the future, Schaake suggests building a public digital stack to compete with private companies and increase government resilience.

Conclusion

Technology policy is not about telling technology companies what to do, or stifling innovation — it's about defending democracy, protecting national security, and economic stability through legitimate, democratic government oversight.

While Big Tech may be a product of democracy, these companies are not democratizing forces. In fact, as we've seen in the past year, these companies are abetting authoritarianism in pursuit of profit. Tech companies are scrambling to squeeze as much protection out of the Trump Administration as possible before Democrats regain power. We've seen it in Elon Musk's takeover and pillaging of federal agencies and their data through DOGE, and more recently in the push for moratoriums on technology regulation — not only domestically, but also through clauses woven into international trade deals and tariff agreements. The Democratic caucus must be prepared on day one of the 120th Congress to reassert the authority of democratically elected officials and begin to roll back Big Tech's control over the American government.
