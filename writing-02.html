Fancy Bear Goes Phishing

A book review arguing that Scott Shapiro's history of major cyberattacks illuminates why the AI race poses a potentially catastrophic security risk — and why cybersecurity is ultimately a political, not a technical, problem.

Type Book Review
Course PPOL6704: Cybersecurity and National Defense Policy
Date April 24, 2026

Scott Shapiro's Fancy Bear Goes Phishing provides readers with a deeper understanding of cybersecurity risks through an exploration of major hacks, worms, and viruses spanning from the Morris Worm in 1988 to the botnet wars of 2016. The case study approach effectively illustrates how hacks, viruses, and worms actually work — how bugs in operating systems are found and exploited, how malicious code spreads from machine to machine, and how simple human error can cause chaos — while also profiling the psychological, economic, and political motivations of the people behind them.

The author goes beyond the technical to explore the legal, political, and cultural contexts in which these hacks take place, arguing that insufficient policy has left private corporations without meaningful accountability for basic cybersecurity standards. This framing is particularly relevant in a policy course. Shapiro argues that the way technology is made is not a foregone conclusion, but rather is shaped by the culture in which it is created. In a culture where there is little to no legal liability for software creators, there is little incentive to prioritize security. As the author states,

Upcode is central to cybersecurity not only because upcode shapes downcode; it also guides how we use downcode. If the upcode lets many users have access to confidential information, grants them rights to change sensitive databases, or puts evil maids in reach of their bosses' cell phones, it does not matter how good the technology is. Without good upcode, good downcode is useless (283).

In an era of corporate consolidation and unenforced antitrust law, the absence of real competition means even reputational damage from a breach isn't enough to compel better security. My main critique of this work, however, is the author's reliance on the "Upcode" and "Downcode" framework, which felt more clever than clarifying and got in the way of his argument.

The section I found most compelling was the discussion of how Microsoft prioritized market capture over cybersecurity in its race to catch up with the internet age, ultimately making its products a major vector for attacks. This dynamic maps closely onto the current AI race, in which companies appear to be shedding security processes and stated moral values in pursuit of market dominance — from shifts in security and privacy standards at major AI labs to changes in OpenAI's corporate structure. As the author argues, profit motives reliably trump public interest, and the absence of government-set security standards leaves us especially exposed. While Microsoft's development of exceedingly vulnerable products in the early 2000s was disruptive, this race to the bottom amongst the AI labs poses a potentially catastrophic risk to societies. The recent news about the impressive capabilities of Anthropic's new Mythos model to find and exploit zero-day vulnerabilities with little user input, and the resulting scramble by national governments around the world, is evidence of this — to say nothing of the incorporation of AI into weapons in pursuit of fully autonomous systems.

Marietje Schaake makes a similar argument in Tech Coup, going a step further by connecting the tension between profit and the public good to the growing trend of governments outsourcing core public functions to private corporations. In an environment defined by a tech oligopoly that is increasingly involved in delivering public services — managing utilities, supplying core government software, shaping elections and wars — the lack of minimum security standards and user protections is a serious vulnerability not only for private use of software, but for the systems our national security relies on.

This tension sits at the heart of the national cybersecurity strategies we examined this semester. The 2026 National Cyber Security Strategy, in particular, calls for a larger role for private companies in securing the nation's digital infrastructure. But as both Shapiro and Schaake argue, and as the behavior of AI companies increasingly demonstrates, we cannot rely on private actors to consistently prioritize the public good over their own power and profit — so we should not be relying on them for our national cybersecurity.

Shapiro ends Fancy Bear Goes Phishing with a critique of "solutionists" — those who think the answer to vulnerabilities in technology is simply better technology — and a call to action for policy leaders.

[S]olutionism eclipses our moral agency and sense of responsibility. Treating security and privacy as mere technical obstacles, solutionists delegate difficult political questions to engineers. Engineers know how computers work. They are technologically literate. But they are also engineers. They are trained to build and operate machines; they are not taught to ponder the ethical costs and consequences of their creations… Cybersecurity is a political decision. Any decision we make must be informed by a deep appreciation of the underlying technology and our fundamental moral values. We cannot hand these decisions off to anyone else (323).

Our leaders must stop deferring to Big Tech and waiting for the engineers and executives at these companies to prioritize the public good over profit. As Shapiro reminds us, cybersecurity is a political decision — and that means it is also a moral one. Legislators must do the difficult work of becoming tech literate enough to write laws that actually shift the incentives driving our vulnerabilities. If the history Shapiro traces teaches us anything, it is that the window between the emergence of a vulnerability and its exploitation is always shorter than we expect, and the cost of inaction is never abstract for long.
